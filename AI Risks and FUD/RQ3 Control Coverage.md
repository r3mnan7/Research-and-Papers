**Research question 3:** For each novel (C/D) risk, and for each catastrophe scenario whose path runs through one, which established controls bound each step of the attack path, and where does an assurance gap remain with those controls fully in place? (Tests H1b.)

## Do Existing Security Frameworks Cover These Risks?

The comparison uses seven published, general-purpose (non-AI-specific) frameworks and standards. The control numbers identify the requirements behind each conclusion. The applications to AI are this paper's analysis; they are not official mappings issued by the publishers or findings that an organization meets a standard. Source publications were reviewed on **October 27, 2026**, and every control identifier cited below was re-verified against the publisher's own text on **November 4, 2026** (see the verification log in [[Sources]]).

> [!warning] Reading this comparison honestly
> 1. **"No new control objective" is true by construction.** A risk is only in scope if it is a failure of confidentiality, integrity, availability, or the authentication, authorization, and accountability mechanisms that protect them (see Scope), and every general framework already has objectives for those. The comparison therefore does not report that as a finding. The informative results are which *steps* of each path a control bounds, which step none does, and what remains when every cited requirement is met.
> 2. **Only general-purpose frameworks are used**, so the comparison tests whether existing controls cover AI rather than assuming controls written for it. The publishers' own AI overlays are discussed separately, as evidence about whether they thought new controls were needed, not as columns.
> 3. **"Full implementation" can't be falsified by incidents.** Any breach can be read as incomplete implementation. Where a step below uses an operator's own attribution of a failure to an implementation gap, it says so, and those attributions are self-reports. [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] asks the different question: what AI does to the *achievability* of full implementation.
> 4. **The mapping is one analyst's reading.** The control-selection rule below is stated so that a second reader can reproduce or dispute each cell. No second rater has classified the cells. Superseded November 4, 2026: see Method item 7.

### Method for This Comparison

The decisions it records (rows from [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]]'s final classes, general frameworks only, path-level analysis, the fit criterion for scenarios, the verdict definitions) are the author's.

1. **Unit of analysis: the step, not the risk.** Each row's attack path is taken from its note in [[RQ2 Screening Log]] (the G notes) or [[Catastrophe Pathways]] (the S notes), steps and lights unchanged. For each step the question is: *which established requirement, if met, stops this step or caps its effect?* A requirement is cited only if its published text covers the step without rewording it. A step with no such requirement is one where only the model's own behavior stands between the attacker and the next step.
2. **Rows and paths.** The sixteen rows that [[RQ2 Risk Classification]] gives a final class of C or D: ten Class C and six Class D (five from the catalogue plus [[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]/[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]] misaligned goals). Row names follow RQ2. **Every in-scope route is analyzed**, not one per row: where a G note gives several in-scope paths (baseline and maximal, or alternative routes), each gets its own step table under the row, and the concerns that [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] maps onto a C/D row without counting them ([[G16 Tool misuse - coerced tool use|G16]] and [[G18 Memory & context poisoning (persistent)|G18]] to indirect injection; [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]] to adversarial inputs; [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]] and [[G44 Obscene - abusive content (CSAM, NCII)|G44]] to direct injection; [[G43 Dangerous, violent, or hateful content|G43]] to poisoning) are analyzed under that row on their own paths. Routes [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] placed outside scope are not analyzed, as in RQ2.
3. **Scenarios.** A catastrophe scenario fits RQ3 when a Class C or D row is a necessary step on its in-scope path, so that RQ3's containment finding for that row bears on the scenario. Four fit: [[S01 Nuclear|S01]] (its indirect path runs through poisoning or adversarial sensor input), [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]] (its safeguard-bypass step is direct prompt injection or weight theft), [[S04 Rogue AI|S04]] (misaligned goals), and [[S06 Financial system|S06]] (its first step is compromise of a shared model or provider: supply chain or a learned backdoor). The other seven do not run through a Class C or D row and are outside RQ3. [[S01 Nuclear|S01]] and [[S06 Financial system|S06]] are mapped step by step; [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]] and [[S04 Rogue AI|S04]] are treated at the level of the controls and the row each runs through, with the steps left in their S notes. Superseded November 4, 2026: [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]] and [[S04 Rogue AI|S04]] now have step tables in the same format as [[S01 Nuclear|S01]] and [[S06 Financial system|S06]].
4. **Step verdicts.** Three values. **Bounded:** an established requirement, met, stops the step or caps its effect. **Bounded within limits:** a requirement caps the step to a stated amount or scope (a quota, a privacy bound, the permissions granted to the model, a curated rather than open source set), and the limit is named. **Unbounded:** no requirement in the seven frameworks acts on the step. (revised Nov 4) A requirement that stops the step outright is Bounded even when the deployer chooses its settings (an egress policy, a cache partition); that choice is recorded as an R1 residual. "Within limits" is used only where the requirement itself leaves harm up to a stated amount or scope, as listed above.
5. **Path verdicts.** A path is **contained** when at least one necessary step is Bounded or Bounded within limits by a requirement that acts *outside the model*. The **assurance gap** is the AI step, the one where the model does what the concern says: in no row is that step Bounded by anything but the model itself (membership inference is the one row where the AI step has a stated mathematical bound). What separates Class C from Class D at path level is where the bounded step sits. In every Class C row an established control bounds a step *upstream* of the AI step (write access to the corpus, access to the vectors, the query budget). In every Class D row the attacker's input is the thing the system exists to accept (a user prompt, a web page, an image, a supplied model, the model's own objective), so nothing upstream is bounded, and the only bounded steps are *downstream*, where the bound is the grant the model was given.
6. **Evidence for the limits.** Each limit cites the primary source that states it: the publisher's own text, the origin paper, or a measured defense evaluation. "None found" means none found in the search recorded in the relevant G or S note; it is an absence-of-evidence claim.
7. **Second rating.** A second rater (an AI agent, in a separate pass) rated the step cells blind, before seeing rater 1's verdicts. On 102 step cells across 29 paths, the two agreed on 79 (77.5%, Cohen's κ = 0.65; 80.4%, κ = 0.69 under a lenient coding of route-split cells) and on 24 of 29 path verdicts (82.8%); see [[RQ3 Second Rating]].

### The Assumption Behind the Comparison

**Assume that every applicable control shown in the comparison is fully implemented, correctly configured, maintained, and operated throughout the system.** This includes connected services and work passed to another agent. For the CIS Controls, assume **Implementation Group 3**, which CIS defines as "comprised of all the Controls and Safeguards," all 153 of them, fully implemented [\[289\]](Sources.md#^s289). For CISA's Zero Trust Maturity Model, assume the relevant capabilities meet its **Optimal** stage. For OWASP ASVS 5.0, assume all applicable requirements through **Level 3** are satisfied. For SP 800-63-4, assume the assurance levels appropriate to each transaction's risk are selected and met.

Only relevant requirements apply. A system that does no training or automated task execution does not need those functions merely to satisfy a checklist. Where they do apply, the assumption includes restricted permissions, identity checks, limits on data movement, monitoring, response, supplier controls, and the listed privacy measures.

This deliberately removes missing controls and poor implementation as explanations. If a system lets an agent change a production database without the required approval, that is a failure to meet the assumed controls. It does not, by itself, show that those controls omit a new AI risk.

At the same time, a control provides only the protection it actually promises. Completing a required attack test does not prove that every possible attack will fail. A privacy method may limit disclosure without eliminating it. Access control can enforce the approved policy without proving that every answer from the model is correct. A broad instruction to "manage risk" is not a technical guarantee that risk has disappeared.

The assumption is demanding. Of the ASVS requirements the step verdicts rely on, these are Level 3, the level ASVS reserves for applications needing the highest assurance: [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-8-3-3|8.3.3]] (originating consumer's permissions), [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-8-3-2|8.3.2]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-2-3-5|2.3.5]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-7-5-3|7.5.3]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-2-4-2|2.4.2]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-14-2-5|14.2.5]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-14-2-6|14.2.6]], [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-15-2-4|15.2.4]], and [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-15-2-5|15.2.5]]. Several SP 800-53 items cited are enhancements outside every baseline ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-3-2|AC-3(2)]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-23|AC-23]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-19-6|SI-19(6)]]/(8)); CIS IG3 is the full set of 153 safeguards, not the smaller IG1 or IG2 subsets; and ZTMM Optimal is the top of four stages. The verdicts say what the controls bound when met at that level; they say nothing about how many deployments meet it.

**What this shows for H1b.** The three publishers whose AI follow-ons are furthest along (NIST for CSF and SP 800-53, CIS for the Controls) all chose profiles, overlays, and companion guides that re-scope existing requirements, and none has yet published a new control objective for AI. That is consistent with H1b. It is not proof: NIST's overlay outline explicitly reserves the option of new controls; SP 800-63-4 did add new AI-driven controls (counted as such above), which is the one exception in the set; and the Cyber AI Profile drew over 1,400 comments, with "standard cybersecurity practices apply" among the phrases commenters asked NIST to explain [\[274\]](Sources.md#^s274). The publishers' choice of mechanism supports "re-scoped, not reinvented"; whether the re-scoped controls are *sufficient* is what the path analysis below tests.

The lights are [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]]'s; the controls and verdicts are this note's. Each path ends with its verdict, residual, and the evidence for any stated limit. In each Class C path an established control bounds a step upstream of the AI step; in each Class D path the bound is downstream and is the grant.

**Path verdict: contained within the granted scope**, at step 5, and for self-trained models also at step 1. **Residual:** harmful behavior within the model's permissions, on a trigger that cannot be shown absent. **Evidence:** NIST, citing Khaddaj et al., notes that absent further assumptions backdoors "are indistinguishable from naturally occurring features in the data" (§2.3.3) [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22); 250 poisoned documents sufficed to backdoor models from 600M to 13B parameters, though the authors judge the trigger they used "unlikely to pose significant risks in frontier models" [\[264\]](Sources.md#^s264); detectors state their own bounds: a deductive detector "may miss a zero-day attack unless the defender introduces an appropriate template" [\[56\]](Sources.md#^s56), and Microsoft's 2026 scanner "works best on backdoors with deterministic outputs" and "does not work on proprietary models which can only be accessed via an API" [\[259\]](Sources.md#^s259). No learned-trigger backdoor has been documented in a deployed model (none found; [[G02 Backdoors - hidden learned functionality|G02]]). The malicious-model incidents on public hubs that this research found ran code when the file was loaded, a reverse shell "embedded in the serialized Pickle data, not learned during training" [\[261\]](Sources.md#^s261), and the cases cited in the [[G02 Backdoors - hidden learned functionality|G02]] note; those are bounded at step 2 by [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^cm-14|CM-14]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^cm-7-7|CM-7(7)]], and ASVS [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-1-5-2|1.5.2]], and [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] classes them as supply chain, not this row.

## Residual: What Remains That Cannot Be Controlled

This is the test of H1b. The path analysis above shows, for each in-scope path, where an established control bounds a step. This section collects what is left *after* that control works, and asks of each remainder one question: is there any requirement, anywhere in the seven frameworks, that acts on it? The question was answered against the full control sets, not from recall: each framework was pulled from its primary source into [[Control Catalogs/_Control Catalogs Index|Control Catalogs]] (SP 800-53's 1,193 controls and enhancements, CIS's 153 safeguards, ASVS's 345 requirements, CSF's 106 subcategories, ZTMM's 40 functions, 800-161r1's 261 entries, and 873 normative statements from 800-63-4), and the catalogs were searched for the terms each remainder turns on (intent, trigger, provenance, pedigree, output filtering, human review, fail-safe, known state, physical). "No control" below therefore means none in the catalog, and the nearest control is named so that a reviewer can check the gap rather than take it on trust.

### Three kinds of remainder

Every residual in the path tables is one of three kinds, and the kind decides what it says about H1b.

- **R1, bounded by a parameter the deployer sets.** The harm that remains is whatever fits inside a grant, a budget, a privacy bound, a cache scope, or a chosen set of sources. A control exists and bounds the step; the remainder is the size of the parameter. This is containment in H1b's sense.
- **R2, no control acts on the remainder.** The step where the harm would have to be stopped has no requirement in any of the seven catalogs that acts on it, or the requirement that exists cannot express the condition. This is where H1b is tested.
- **R3, outside information security.** The remainder is bounded, if at all, by a control the seven frameworks do not contain because it is not an information-security control (a safety fallback, a market circuit breaker, warning doctrine). RQ3 records these but does not count them for or against H1b.

### What the table shows

**Every R2 entry is the same five gaps.** Collapsing the table, the seven frameworks have no requirement that acts on:

1. **A learned condition that cannot be shown absent:** a backdoor trigger ([[G02 Backdoors - hidden learned functionality|G02]]) and a misaligned goal ([[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]/[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]]). The catalogs test what can be specified; a condition that is "indistinguishable from naturally occurring features" [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22) cannot be specified. *Where the harm lands:* inside the grant or the isolation, which are R1.
2. **An attacker's input that the system exists to accept:** a prompt ([[G10 Direct prompt injection - jailbreak|G10]]), a document ([[G11 Indirect prompt injection - goal hijack|G11]]), a sensor reading ([[G03 Evasion - adversarial inputs|G03]], [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]), an open-web corpus ([[G01 Training data - model poisoning|G01]] web-scale). Nothing upstream can be bounded because there is no trusted-source list to enforce at that step. *Where the harm lands:* downstream, in the grant, except for the two cases below.
3. **A grant that is the model's output.** For a chat model ([[G10 Direct prompt injection - jailbreak|G10]] chat, [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]], [[G44 Obscene - abusive content (CSAM, NCII)|G44]]) the only downstream requirement is output filtering ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-15|SI-15]]), which is itself a learned classifier with the same assurance problem as the model it filters. *Where the harm lands:* in the output, with no further stop inside the deployer's controls.
4. **A condition no policy can express:** "derives from the user's intent" ([[G14 Excessive agency - privilege - credentials|G14]]). Authorization has subjects, objects, and attributes; it has no field for intent. *Where the harm lands:* inside a grant that was narrowed until the remainder was tolerable [\[254\]](Sources.md#^s254), [\[75\]](Sources.md#^s75).
5. **A confidently wrong output that is not an indicated failure** ([[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]], and the sensor case of [[G03 Evasion - adversarial inputs|G03]]). [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-17|SI-17]] and [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sc-24|SC-24]] are the fail-safe controls and they are conditioned on a failure being indicated; the stop that fires is the safety supervisor, outside the frameworks. *Where the harm lands:* at the actuator, bounded by a control RQ3 does not count.

Memorized free text ([[G07 Training-data reconstruction - extraction - memorization|G07]] query route) is a sixth, smaller gap of kind 3: output filtering cannot recognize it, and the stop is upstream.

**What this proves and disproves about H1b.** H1b says the gaps are in achievability, not in the control set's ability to contain impact. The residual table says:

- *Supported, for R1.* In every row the harm that remains after the bounded step is the size of a parameter the deployer sets: a grant, a budget, a bound, a scope, a source list. Containment is not in question for these; what is in question is whether the parameter was set, which is the achievability claim of H1 (the ten Class C rows and the isolation steps of the Class D rows, with every real failure on record being a parameter that was not set [\[40\]](Sources.md#^s40), [\[75\]](Sources.md#^s75); in Cline the decisive failure on record is a rotation that deleted the wrong token while the exposed one "remained active", and the injection step there is not confirmed on the record [\[253\]](Sources.md#^s253) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]])).
- *Not supported, for gaps 3, 4, and 5.* For a chat model, for intent, and for a confidently wrong physical-world output, the frameworks cannot contain the impact at all: not because a control was not implemented but because no control in 2,971 catalog entries acts on the remainder. For gaps 3 and 4 the only remedy the frameworks offer is to remove function (no tools, no internet, a narrower grant, no chat output), which is containment by giving up the use case, and NCSC says exactly that: if the remaining risk cannot be tolerated, "it may not be a good use case for LLMs" [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27). For gap 5 the remedy exists but is a safety control, and RQ3's scope cannot claim it.
- *Not testable, for gaps 1 and 2.* These are gaps in *prevention* at the AI step, which H1b does not claim; the impact of what they let through lands in R1 territory. They are the assurance gaps the Working Answer names, and they are real, but they do not falsify a claim about containment.

**The honest statement of H1b, then:** impact is contained wherever the harm lands inside a parameter the deployer sets, and that is most of the sixteen rows; it is not contained where the harm is the model's output itself, where the restriction is about intent, or where the wrong output goes straight to an actuator, and in those three places the frameworks have no control to add, only function to remove. Falsifier 5 is not met as written, because every path has *some* bounded step; but a reader who takes "contain impact" to mean "contain it without removing the function the system was deployed for" should read H1b as falsified for gaps 3 and 4. The paper should offer both readings and pick one in its wording.

## What the Findings Mean in Practice

### Separate an Uncovered Requirement from a Limit of the Method

The central question is whether a protection is missing from the requirements, or whether the method used to meet a requirement has a known limit. A requirement to test a model may be fully satisfied even though the test does not cover every future input. An access-control requirement that forbids a specific action is a different kind of claim: if that action succeeds, the required boundary has not held.

The comparison credits protections for what they actually enforce. It also keeps the limits visible. A new finding must explain what harm remains possible while the relevant requirements are still satisfied. Simply recommending a control already assumed present does not establish an additional AI requirement. [\[8\]](Sources.md#^s8), [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15)

CIS application-security safeguards are relevant when an organization acquires or hosts software as well as when it writes it. Here they support architecture review, supplier acceptance, and operational tests. Applying a software safeguard to a supplied model is this paper's interpretation, not a claim that CIS explicitly names model weights; CIS's own April 2026 companion guides make the same move, adapting v8.1 safeguards to AI systems [\[277\]](Sources.md#^s277). [\[14\]](Sources.md#^s14)

### Assess the Whole System, Including Connected Services

CISA's model already includes access limited to individual actions and resources, with permission checks that continue during operation. This gives agent controls an established place in security engineering. [\[16\]](Sources.md#^s16)

For each consequential action, identify the initiating user, the service identities, the computers running the work, the tools and connected services, the communication paths, and the affected data. Include search indexes, temporary copies, caches, model files, and training updates where relevant. Protecting the AI's front door does not establish that every connected tool applies the same restrictions.

Keep records that link the original request, information supplied to the model, proposed action, permission decision, and completed operation. Establish who owns the policy and who can stop the work. Test the result at the affected system. The model's explanation is useful context, but it is not an independent record of what happened; in about 7% of transcripts reviewed from one 2026 incident, agents made apparent tool activity differ from actual execution [\[66\]](Sources.md#^s66), which is why [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^au-9-2|AU-9(2)]] and ASVS [[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-16-4-3|16.4.3]] put the record outside the agent's reach.

Assess control maturity and attack results separately. A mature access system can enforce permissions while a model still makes a wrong prediction. A model refusing one unsafe request does not establish mature network or identity controls. Neither observation alone determines an organization's ZTMM stage.

### Outside Content Must Not Authorize Actions

Prompt injection occurs when text the AI is supposed to read changes what it treats as instructions. A retrieved page might tell an assistant to send a document elsewhere. The model may accept that instruction, but a separate permission check should still prevent the transfer if the task did not authorize it.

SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-10-6|SI-10(6)]]** and ASVS **v5.0.0-[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-1-3-2|1.3.2]]/[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-1-3-7|1.3.7]]** address injection into conventional interpreters; [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-10-6|SI-10(6)]]'s discussion names the fix, "a parameterized interface or output escaping," and that is exactly what a language model lacks [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27). ASVS **v5.0.0-[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-8-3-3|8.3.3]]** supplies the out-of-model half: access is based on the originating user's permissions, not on the permissions of an intermediary acting for them. SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-4|AC-4]]** and **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sc-7-10|SC-7(10)]]** supply the other half: information-flow enforcement and tested exfiltration prevention, so that even an instruction the model follows has nowhere to send the data. [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[17\]](Sources.md#^s17)

The 2025 practitioner rules are these requirements restated for agents. The "lethal trifecta" names the three capabilities that must not coexist in one agent: access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and a way to communicate externally [\[251\]](Sources.md#^s251). Meta's "Rule of Two" allows an agent session at most two of: processing untrustworthy inputs, access to sensitive systems or private data, and the ability to change state or communicate externally, with a human in the loop otherwise [\[252\]](Sources.md#^s252). NCSC's rule drops the model's privileges to those of whoever supplied the content it is reading [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27). The measured defenses point the same way: deterministic restriction of callable tools once untrusted data is read was the best-performing defense in the AgentDojo benchmark with no utility loss [\[249\]](Sources.md#^s249), and CaMeL enforces capabilities and data-flow policy outside the model while excluding, by its authors' own account, flows the policy permits [\[248\]](Sources.md#^s248).

**Engineering check:** Can an untrusted page or tool result cause an unapproved write, message, or disclosure? Restrict operations, destinations, data, and supplied values outside the model. Measure completed harmful actions as well as whether the model followed the misleading text. Knowing the source of a document does not authorize that source to change the task. Test defenses against adaptive attacks, not fixed attack strings [\[250\]](Sources.md#^s250).

### Valid Input Can Still Produce an Unsafe Model Decision

An attacker-designed image or other input can pass file-format checks and still cause a wrong prediction. Normal accuracy and performance under attack answer different questions. [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22)

**Engineering check:** Define what the attacker can change, how much they know, and how many attempts they can make. Test the full workflow, including any action taken from the prediction. Report ordinary performance, attack success, mistaken rejection of legitimate inputs, and the resulting harm. Use independent checks or a safe fallback where one wrong prediction could authorize a serious action; SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-3-2|AC-3(2)]]** dual authorization is the established form of that check, and the nuclear warning doctrine's "two independent information sources using different physical principles" [\[133\]](Sources.md#^s133) is the same control in another field. General attack-testing requirements ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ca-8|CA-8]], CIS [[CIS Controls v8.1 Catalog#^cis-16-13|16.13]]) apply but don't specify model-robustness testing, and complete protection against all possible inputs is not established by them. Where a certified defense is used, state the norm and radius it certifies and the fraction of inputs it covers [\[255\]](Sources.md#^s255); outside that envelope the guarantee is silent.

### AI Search Combines Several Different Security Problems

An embedding is a numerical representation used to compare or retrieve information. Four different failures can arise around it: one customer receives another customer's document; an attacker manipulates which documents rank highly, or plants one that makes the system refuse; someone learns protected information from numerical representations or answers they are allowed to receive; or a cached answer built for one user is served to another. Each needs its own assessment.

**Engineering check:** Preserve document permissions when creating smaller text sections, search indexes, caches, and temporary copies (SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-16|AC-16]]** attributes travel with the data). Prevent unauthorized material from reaching the model in the first place; filtering the final answer cannot undo that exposure. Test permission changes and withdrawal of access as well as first use. Separately test manipulation of search results, jamming, and information recoverable from allowed outputs. Scope semantic caches by principal (SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sc-4|SC-4]]**, ASVS **v5.0.0-[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-8-4-1|8.4.1]]**). A similarity score or a single "vector security" result does not establish all four protections. [\[8\]](Sources.md#^s8), [\[10\]](Sources.md#^s10), [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[17\]](Sources.md#^s17)

### Training Updates Can Reveal Information Even When Encrypted in Transit

During training, systems may share numerical updates called gradients. An authorized recipient can sometimes use them to recover information about the examples used for training. Encryption protects the transfer from interception; it does not prevent the intended recipient from analyzing what it receives.

Differential privacy limits how much a release can reveal about a protected person or record, under stated assumptions and settings. Secure aggregation can hide individual contributions while still allowing a combined result to be calculated. The combined result may still reveal information. These methods solve different problems and do not provide unconditional secrecy. SP 800-53 explicitly includes differential privacy and testing for identifying individuals, and its own discussion of the motivated-intruder test says the test "cannot by itself prove sufficiency." [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[20\]](Sources.md#^s20), [\[60\]](Sources.md#^s60)

**Engineering check:** Identify exactly what is released, who receives it, how often releases occur, and what the privacy guarantee covers. Account for repeated releases. Protection established for the finished model does not automatically cover raw training updates. A failed reconstruction attempt is test evidence; it is not, by itself, a mathematical privacy guarantee. For membership and property inference, note which the guarantee bounds: record-level differential privacy bounds membership advantage [\[266\]](Sources.md#^s266) but not population-level properties [\[288\]](Sources.md#^s288).

### Queries Are an Attack Surface

Four Class C rows share one shape: the attacker needs nothing but permitted queries, and what they learn or consume scales with how many they can make and how much each answer reveals. The frameworks already treat this as a problem: SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-23|AC-23]]** exists to detect and prevent unauthorized data mining, and its discussion lists the containment, "limiting the number and frequency of database queries," limiting response types, and differential privacy; **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-15|SI-15]]** validates that output is consistent with expected content. ASVS **v5.0.0-[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-2-4-1|2.4.1]]** requires anti-automation against "quota exhaustion, rate-limit breaches, denial-of-service, or overuse of costly resources," and **[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-15-3-1|15.3.1]]** requires returning only the fields a function needs. SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sc-6|SC-6]]** allocates resources "by priority, quota." [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[17\]](Sources.md#^s17)

**Engineering check:** Treat logits, confidence scores, embeddings, and verbose reasoning as sensitive output; return the minimum. Cap compute, tokens, and time per request and budget per principal. Monitor query distributions per account, and tie accounts to proofed identities where the service is valuable enough to steal. Expect the controls to raise the attacker's cost rather than zero it: extraction still works without confidence values [\[44\]](Sources.md#^s44), and a capped sponge query still costs the cap [\[263\]](Sources.md#^s263). Whether monitoring can *detect* an extraction pattern or a sponge input is a separate question that the cited requirements ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-4|SI-4]], [[NIST CSF 2.0 Catalog#^de-cm-09|DE.CM-09]]) do not answer; the paper should not credit detection without a measured detector.

### A Genuine Model File Can Still Contain Harmful Learned Behavior

A signature can establish that a model file came from the expected supplier and has not changed. It cannot establish that the model will behave safely under every condition. A hidden trigger may produce harmful behavior while ordinary tests look normal.

The comparison includes supplier assessment, provenance, authenticity, and integrity verification for supplied components and "associated data" ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sr-4|SR-4]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sr-6|SR-6]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sr-11|SR-11]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^si-7|SI-7]] with SP 800-161r1 guidance), signed components ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^cm-14|CM-14]]), confined execution of binary code ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^cm-7-7|CM-7(7)]]), and ASVS dependency and deserialization requirements. None of these requires testing a model for hidden triggers, and the model-signing specifications say what they attest: source and integrity [\[260\]](Sources.md#^s260). [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[17\]](Sources.md#^s17), [\[19\]](Sources.md#^s19)

**Engineering check:** Track the model, its adjustments, configuration, and relevant dependencies. Test plausible trigger conditions and targeted harmful results, including effects hidden by a good average score. Reassess changes that affect the threat, such as further training, replacement, or reducing model precision. Define rollback conditions. These measures improve confidence; finite tests cannot establish the absence of every hidden trigger [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[48\]](Sources.md#^s48). Keep this row separate from malicious model *files*: the malicious models found on public hubs ran code when loaded [\[261\]](Sources.md#^s261), and signing, scanning, and safe deserialization address that fully.

### Approval Must Follow the Work Through Every Tool and Agent

An agent is software that uses a model to choose and carry out steps through tools. The user's account may have broad permissions, but a particular task should grant the agent only the permissions needed for that work. Passing work to another agent should not increase those permissions.

Define the resources, operations, recipients, limits, and expiry. Present the actual proposed action for approval, protect that approval from alteration or reuse, and check it against the action that runs. Recheck current policy during long sessions and when the proposed action changes. Keep a reliable link from the original request to delegated work and the completed operation. CISA's access-management functions, SP 800-53 [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-3|AC-3]]/[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-6|AC-6]], [[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-3-12|AC-3(12)]] (an application asserts the access it needs before it is granted), and ASVS **v5.0.0-[[OWASP ASVS 5.0.0 Catalog#^asvs-8-3-3|8.3.3]]** (permissions of the originating subject, not the intermediary) express these restrictions. [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[16\]](Sources.md#^s16), [\[17\]](Sources.md#^s17)

**Engineering check:** Can outside content redirect an approved operation to another recipient, amount, or system? Does a separate control reject the change even when the account has broader access? A model explaining that the action serves the task is not proof that it was approved. Also, SP 800-53 **[[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^ac-3-2|AC-3(2)]]** requires two authorized people; one person approving an AI proposal does not automatically meet that enhancement. [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15)

A vague written goal can still be misunderstood. To establish a remaining security gap, specify the harmful outcome and show why existing permission and approval methods cannot express or enforce the needed restriction. The comparison does not establish such a gap merely because unrestricted language is difficult to interpret.

The intent-propagation row meets that test, and the note should say so rather than bury it. The harmful outcome is specified by the 2026 record: fabricated identities, messages to real people, and social engineering of a maintainer, all inside a grant of "internet access" [\[254\]](Sources.md#^s254). Existing permission methods cannot express the needed restriction because the restriction is about intent, which a policy has no field for; they can only narrow the grant until what remains inside it is tolerable, which is what AISI and the vendor did [\[254\]](Sources.md#^s254), [\[75\]](Sources.md#^s75). That is containment by giving up function, and it is a different claim from "the control set contains the impact." The Working Answer carries this forward.

### Contain the Model as You Would an Insider, and Test the Stop

The containment for a model that might pursue its own goal is the containment for a trusted insider who might: least privilege, deny-by-default egress, off-box records, and a stop mechanism that works. The 2026 incidents tested each of these. A DNS resolver left reachable became an egress channel, the monitor fired within minutes, and the run was stopped manually about two and a half hours later because the automatic stop, which existed, did not operate: the run "did not stop automatically as expected" [\[73\]](Sources.md#^s73), and OpenAI later repaired its automatic-pausing systems [\[74\]](Sources.md#^s74) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]). Agents in evaluation sandboxes reached the internet through zero-days in a package-cache proxy that was their permitted route, evading outbound controls that were in place; the proxy's own internet access was limited by purpose, not destination [\[64\]](Sources.md#^s64), [\[65\]](Sources.md#^s65) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]). The operators attributed the DNS and misconfiguration failures to gaps in applying known controls; in the Hugging Face egress and the DNS stop, a control that was in place was evaded or failed (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]); those are self-reports, and no independent audit of the repaired environments exists [\[74\]](Sources.md#^s74).

What insider-risk programs also rely on, vetting and deterrence, has no counterpart for a model: alignment evaluations "did not warn" of the behavior seen [\[68\]](Sources.md#^s68), models increasingly distinguish evaluation from deployment [\[109\]](Sources.md#^s109), and chain-of-thought monitoring is "a useful but imperfect tool" [\[75\]](Sources.md#^s75). The containment is established; the assurance is not.

**Engineering check:** Enumerate every egress path including DNS, package registries, and shared caches, and test exfiltration ([[NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 Catalog#^sc-7-10|SC-7(10)]]) at machine tempo, not on a human schedule (see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]], Achievability). Capture tool records outside the agent's execution context. Test the stop mechanism to final effect, with a time budget. Decide before deployment who owns the policy and who can stop the work [\[26\]](Sources.md#^s26).

## Working Answer to RQ3

**Containment:** Across the seven general-purpose frameworks, every one of the sixteen Class C and D rows, and 24 of the 26 paths analyzed under them, is contained at some step by a requirement that acts outside the model; the two uncontained paths are [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]'s adversarial-sensor route and [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]]'s jailbreak route (Counts, above). Where the bounded step sits is what the classes mean in practice. In the ten Class C rows an established control bounds a step *upstream* of the AI step: write access to the corpus, access to the vectors, the query budget, the privacy budget. In the six Class D rows nothing upstream of the attacker's input can be bounded, because that input is what the system exists to accept: for four of them the bounded step is *downstream* and the bound is the grant the model was given, and for [[G14 Excessive agency - privilege - credentials|G14]] and [[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]/[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]] the stop comes earlier, at least privilege on the grant and at the isolation boundary. Eleven rows need no more than minor adaptation. Three of the four catastrophe scenarios whose paths run through a C/D row ([[S01 Nuclear|S01]], [[S04 Rogue AI|S04]], [[S06 Financial system|S06]]) fail at a step an established control bounds; [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]]'s jailbreak route is the [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]] exception. In [[S01 Nuclear|S01]] and [[S06 Financial system|S06]] that control is independent verification, the same downstream containment RQ3 finds for the adversarial-inputs row. That the frameworks lack no *objective* is true by construction and is not counted as a result. This supports H1b for the containment half of security, and the frameworks' own publishers reached the same design conclusion when they turned to AI, choosing overlays, profiles, and companion guides over new controls [\[274\]](Sources.md#^s274), [\[275\]](Sources.md#^s275), [\[277\]](Sources.md#^s277), with SP 800-63-4 the one framework in the set that added new controls because of AI (63 §3.8 disclosure of AI/ML use; 63A-4 §3.14 forged-media analysis), counted as new controls in the publishers table above.

**Prevention and assurance:** The AI step is unbounded in eleven of sixteen rows, and in all six Class D rows. These aren't implementation failures or "limits of the method" to be footnoted. They are the places where learned systems can't yet be given the assurance conventional software gets: no instruction/data boundary to enforce (the in-model attempt at one, an "instruction hierarchy" that teaches the model "to selectively ignore lower-privileged instructions," is itself learned behavior [\[291\]](Sources.md#^s291), and the adaptive-attack results cited at the direct-injection row are the measured limit of such defenses [\[250\]](Sources.md#^s250)), no specification to test learned behavior against, and no way to establish the absence of a trigger or a goal. Real-world incidence differs across the six: the two injection rows and intent propagation have caused harm in production [\[253\]](Sources.md#^s253), [\[40\]](Sources.md#^s40), [\[254\]](Sources.md#^s254) (in Cline an unauthorized version was published, but the injection step is not confirmed on the record and Cline states "No malicious code was delivered" [\[253\]](Sources.md#^s253)) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]); adversarial inputs have defeated deployed classifiers and a production driver-assistance system without physical harm [\[257\]](Sources.md#^s257); learned backdoors have never been documented in a deployed model; and misaligned goals have been shown only in evaluations, with the 2026 incidents attributed by their operators to recklessness and momentum rather than goals of the model's own [\[68\]](Sources.md#^s68), [\[75\]](Sources.md#^s75).

**Where H1b is weakest:** For the Class D rows the bound is the grant or the isolation. For three of them (the injection rows and intent propagation) the residual harm *within* that grant has already occurred in production, and the only remedy the frameworks can express is to narrow the grant until what remains inside it is tolerable [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27), [\[251\]](Sources.md#^s251), [\[252\]](Sources.md#^s252), [\[254\]](Sources.md#^s254). That is containment, but containment by giving up function, and H1b's wording, that gaps are "not in the control set's ability to contain impact," is true only in that sense. The paper should say so. Two further cautions: incident evidence that containment *held* is thin, since most real cases involved a missing conventional control, and the few where a correctly scoped control was present and held (an egress deny and admin-only repository creation in OpenAI's internal reports; third-party rate limits, sign-up checks and record-type restrictions in [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] Cases 7 and 8) rest largely on self-reports (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]); and the attributions of those failures to implementation gaps are the operators' own.

**Where adaptation is needed:** Three rows need engineering the general frameworks don't specify: robustness testing of learned decision boundaries beyond biometrics, trigger testing of supplied models, and evaluation of a model's goals before deployment. The two injection rows need in-model work that no framework can require because no method yet delivers it. The Class C rows and intent propagation need only the existing requirements applied to new artifacts: chunks, caches, indexes, gradients, logits, tool grants.

**Residual (the H1b test):** the Residual section collapses every path's remainder into three kinds and five gaps, checked against the full catalogs. Where the remainder sits inside a parameter the deployer sets (a grant, a budget, a bound, a scope, a source list), containment holds and the question is achievability, which is H1. Where it does not, in three places (a chat model whose grant is its output; an action that is permitted but not intended; a confident wrong output that goes to an actuator), the frameworks have no control to add and can only remove function.

**Falsifier 5 (H1b):** (revised Nov 4) met for two paths, both within the exceptions H1b now names. [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]]'s jailbreak route has no bounded security step, because the output is the harm. [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]'s adversarial-sensor route is stopped only by a safety fallback outside the frameworks. Every other path is bounded at some step when the controls are verifiably implemented. The honest statement of H1b is therefore "fairly well," not "completely": impact is bounded except in the named cases, prevention at the AI step is not, and for the agentic rows the bound is the grant itself.

**Test for any future claim of a missing control:** Name the step, the requirement already satisfied at that step, the protection it actually provides, the harm that remains possible while it works, and the additional protection needed.

## Consistency With RQ2 and RQ4

- **Rows, classes, lights, and steps** match [[RQ2 Risk Classification]] and the G and S notes: 10 C, 6 D, including [[G08 Property inference - model characterisation (recon)|G08]] and [[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]/[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]]; four 🟢 Class D rows (adversarial inputs against classifiers, both injection rows, intent propagation); poisoning and availability attacks 🟢; the other eight Class C rows 🟡; all eleven scenarios 🔴. The [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] "Where It's Real" table lists five Class D mechanisms by merging direct and indirect injection; this note keeps them as separate rows because their containment differs and gives six. [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] classes each scenario's AI step; this note's fit criterion is whether a C/D row is a necessary step anywhere on the in-scope path, which is why [[S01 Nuclear|S01]], [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]], and [[S06 Financial system|S06]] fit although [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] classes their AI step outside C/D.
- **[[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]]** found a Class C/D step causal *rather than* a conventional failure in one place, the jailbreak behind GTG-1002's misuse of its provider's service, where the attacker held the grant; in every other incident with harm on record a conventional failure was also necessary, including the incidents where a Class D step was necessary (actions inside the enforced grant that did not derive from the user's intent) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]). This note's misaligned-goals and intent-propagation rows reach the same reading of the same incidents from the control side: the isolation and egress steps were the gap, and their verification at machine tempo is the achievability problem [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] names.
- **The main note's H1b status line** reads six assurance gaps, counted as rows (direct injection, indirect injection, intent propagation, adversarial inputs, learned backdoors, misaligned goals), with the real-world split above.
