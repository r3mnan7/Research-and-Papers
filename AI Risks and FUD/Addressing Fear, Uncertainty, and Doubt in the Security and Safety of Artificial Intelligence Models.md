## Working Hypothesis

Wording, the objective-level argument, falsification criteria, and the status lines were AI-drafted. H1.1–H1.3 were drafted with AI assistance in the author's voice, from his stated hypothesis and the [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] discussion, for his review. The hypotheses themselves are his.

**H1 (this paper):** Most of what people fear about AI security is the same set of problems security has always dealt with. Where that stops being true depends on the role AI plays in the risk, so the hypothesis is stated for each role.
- **H1.1 — Attackers using AI:** When attackers use AI, they run attacks we already know, but faster (**tempo**), more often (**frequency**), and more cheaply (**cost to the attacker**). AI changes the economics of the attack, not its kind.
- **H1.2 — AI as a faulty component:** When AI fails inside our own systems, it is an unreliable component we trusted further than we should have. The model is the new part; the failure, and the controls that address it, are ones we already use once they are re-scoped to the model.
- **H1.3 — Attacks on AI systems:** When attackers go after the AI itself, some of what they do is an old problem moved onto a new artifact, such as model weights, vector stores, or agent tool chains, and some of it is genuinely new. Established controls contain most of the new mechanisms. A short list remains where we can contain the risk but cannot yet get assurance against it, and the paper names that list rather than minimizing it.
- *Current status from [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]]:* H1.1 holds on classification: 9 of 9 are Class A. Measured data show the amplification most clearly in attacker cost; tempo and frequency trends are real but not yet attributed to AI by the organizations measuring them. H1.2 holds: 10 of 11 are Class A or B. H1.3 holds: 12 of 27 attacks on AI systems are established (9 of 23 on the catalogue), 22 of 27 are Class A, B, or C, and five are Class D ([[RQ2 Risk Classification]], Tally by AI's Role).
- *Current status from [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]]:* (Nov 5, 2026) Across nine full cases and 137 register entries (November 2023 to November 2026), falsifier 2 is not met wherever something outside the model stood on the path: every such harm on record also required a conventional control that was missing, misconfigured, or defeated through a conventional flaw, so no Class C/D step was causal *rather than* a conventional failure. Read literally, it is met once: the jailbreak behind GTG-1002's misuse of its provider's service, where the attacker was the deployer and nothing outside the model stood on the path. The author counts that misuse as in scope, so H1.3 does not hold for that narrow class; it is the route [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] already marks unbounded and the condition H1b names as uncontained. A second literal hit, Freysa, is out of scope: the model was the only control by design and the payout was the game's advertised prize. The author has settled that an agent's action inside its enforced grant but against the user's intent is Class D; on that reading eighteen incidents with harm on record have a necessary C/D step, and in all but GTG-1002's provider-side harm a conventional failure was also necessary. The record mostly shows controls absent rather than present and holding, and rests largely on operator self-reports ([[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]], Working Answer).

**H1b (control sufficiency):** Today's established frameworks and controls cover AI risk fairly well, if not completely. That set is CSF 2.0, SP 800-53, the CIS Controls, ZTMM, OWASP ASVS, SP 800-63-4, and SP 800-161r1, none of them written for AI. The gaps are in prevention and assurance *inside* the model. The control set contains impact wherever the harm has to pass through something the deployer sets outside the model: a grant, a budget, a privacy bound, a source list, an isolation boundary. It cannot contain impact where nothing outside the model stands between the model and the harm: a model whose output is itself the harm, an action that is permitted but not intended, and a confidently wrong output that drives an actuator. There, the frameworks can reduce impact only by removing the function.
- *Current status from [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]]:* (revised Nov 4, after the [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]]/[[S04 Rogue AI|S04]] tables and the second rating) Containment holds for all 16 Class C/D rows and for 24 of the 26 paths analyzed under them: each is bounded at some step by a requirement that acts outside the model. In the ten Class C rows that step is upstream of the AI step. In the six Class D rows it is downstream: for four of them the bound is the grant, while [[G14 Excessive agency - privilege - credentials|G14]] and [[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]/[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]] are stopped earlier, by least privilege and by isolation. 11 rows need at most minor adaptation. (That no control *objective* is missing is true by construction and is not counted.) The two uncontained paths are two of the three exceptions H1b names. [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]]'s jailbreak route has no bounded security step, because the output is the harm. [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]'s adversarial-sensor route sends a confident wrong output to an actuator, and its only stop is a safety fallback outside the frameworks. The third exception, intent, is contained only by narrowing the grant. Falsifier 5 is met for those two paths, which is why H1b now names its exceptions instead of claiming containment everywhere (see Known limitations). Three of the four catastrophe scenarios that fit [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] fail at a step an established control bounds; [[S02 Bio-chemical|S02]]'s jailbreak route is the [[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]] exception ([[RQ3 Control Coverage]], Working Answer and Residual). Six assurance gaps remain, counted as rows: direct prompt injection, indirect prompt injection, agentic intent propagation, adversarial inputs, learned backdoors, and misaligned goals. (The paper counts the two injection rows separately because their containment differs; [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]]'s "Where It's Real" table shows them as one row.) Of the six, the two injection rows and intent propagation have caused harm in production; adversarial inputs have defeated deployed classifiers and a production driver-assistance system without physical harm; learned backdoors have never been documented in a deployed model; misaligned goals are shown only in evaluations ([[RQ2 Screening Log]]: [[G02 Backdoors - hidden learned functionality|G02]], [[G03 Evasion - adversarial inputs|G03]], [[G10 Direct prompt injection - jailbreak|G10]]–[[G11 Indirect prompt injection - goal hijack|G11]], [[G14 Excessive agency - privilege - credentials|G14]], [[G56 Existential risk - loss of control over advanced AI - superintelligenc|G56]]–[[G57 Misaligned AI goals (proxy gaming, goal drift, power-seeking, deceptio|G57]]). A blind second rating of [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]]'s step cells agreed on 79 of 102 cells (77.5%, κ = 0.65), rater 1 being the author and rater 2 an AI agent working blind ([[RQ3 Second Rating]]). The evidence supports "fairly well." It does not support "completely," and the paper should say that plainly. The frameworks' own publishers reached the same design conclusion when they turned to AI: overlays, profiles and companion guides, not new controls, with one exception: SP 800-63-4 added new mandatory AI-driven requirements (AI/ML disclosure; forged-media analysis in remote proofing), which the paper counts as new controls ([[RQ3 Control Coverage]], "What the Publishers Themselves Say About AI").

**H0 (the FUD position being tested):** AI introduces a new class of security risk that established principles and controls cannot address, so security must be reinvented for AI.

**What would falsify H1:** Each test applies to its own group, never to the combined count.
1. *(H1.1)* Fewer than half of the attackers-using-AI risks are Class A. *(H1.2)* Fewer than half of the faulty-component risks are Class A or B. *(H1.3)* Fewer than half of the attacks on AI systems are Class A, B, or C.
2. *(H1.3)* Class C/D mechanisms are the *causal* step in real incidents, rather than conventional failures ([[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]]).
3. *(H1.3)* Established controls, verifiably in place, fail to contain a Class C risk.
4. *(H1.1)* Threat intelligence shows AI giving attackers new capabilities rather than faster or cheaper versions of existing ones ([[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] amplification evidence).
5. *(H1b)* A risk whose **impact** established controls cannot contain even when they're verifiably implemented, as opposed to a risk they contain but can't prevent inside the model ([[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]]).

## Classification Rubric (applied in RQ2, RQ3, RQ4)

| Class | Name | Test | Counts toward H1? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Amplified | Established mechanism and target. AI changes tempo, frequency, scale, cost, or the skill needed. | Yes. This is the core claim. |
| **B** | Relocated | Established mechanism applied to a new artifact or location (weights, embeddings, prompts, agent credentials). Existing controls apply once re-scoped. | Yes. It's the same problem in a new place. |
| **C** | Novel mechanism, contained | The mechanism exploits a property of *learned* representations, with no conventional software flaw. Controls outside the model (least privilege, isolation, differential privacy, provenance) still bound the impact. | Partly. New attack, old containment. |
| **D** | Novel mechanism, assurance gap | Same as C, but no current method gives assurance comparable to the conventional counterpart. Residual risk remains with controls fully in place. | No. This is the "Reality" side of FUD vs. Reality. |

Distinctions applied consistently:
- **Mechanism vs. trust model vs. control requirement.** A deepfake defeats an *informal* identity signal (a familiar voice). The control, independent verification, is established, so it's Class A, not a new trust model. Agents that never tire remove the same implicit defender assumption (attacker effort is costly), so they are also Class A.
- **Model error vs. model exploitation.** A model being wrong, with no attacker and no security control defeated, is outside scope (see Scope). Where a model's error defeats a security control or produces an action outside what was authorized (security-AI misclassification, agents exceeding scope, weapons engaging outside authorized parameters), it is Class B: an unreliable component trusted downstream, with the model as the new component. Harm where an adversary exploits learned behavior to cause the wrong output is Class C or D. Pursuing a goal is not an error, so misaligned goals stay Class D. Correlated behavior across many models that are each working as built is amplification (Class A), not error. This rule was made explicit after the first classification pass to resolve inconsistent rows.
  - *Clarification (author's decision, Nov 5, 2026):* "authorized" in this rule means permitted by the enforced controls (the grant), not what the agent was instructed. A model's action inside its enforced grant that does not derive from the user's intent is Class D ([[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]]'s Agentic Authority / Intent Propagation row); an action that defeats a control or goes outside the enforced grant is Class B ([[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]).
- **Containment ≠ prevention.** A Class C/D risk can be fully contained by an external control and still be unprevented inside the model. Both facts get reported.
- **Origin dates matter.** Several "novel" mechanisms were published 2013–2019 (adversarial examples [\[42\]](Sources.md#^s42), model extraction [\[44\]](Sources.md#^s44), membership inference [\[43\]](Sources.md#^s43), gradient leakage [\[45\]](Sources.md#^s45), backdoors [\[47\]](Sources.md#^s47)). Novel to ML ≠ new in 2026.

## Scope

This paper focuses on operational systems security. Software-development risks, such as AI-generated code, are included where they meet the downstream test below.

The analysis is limited to concrete cybersecurity risk. Broader social, political, ethical, and speculative concerns are included only where they meet the downstream test below.

A claimed AI risk is in scope when the harm cannot happen without a failure of the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an information system or the information it handles, including failure of the authentication, authorization, and accountability mechanisms that protect them. This follows the statutory definition of information security, 44 U.S.C. § 3552(b)(3) [\[6\]](Sources.md#^s6). Each route to the harm is tested with three questions: what system or information fails, which of those properties fails, and whether that failure is necessary on the route. A concern is In if at least one route passes and Out if none does; there is no partial verdict. Only the routes that pass are analyzed. The others are recorded, with their lights, in [[RQ2 Screening Log]]. Paths can be possibilities, not only what is documented today.
- **Integrity** means "guarding against improper information modification or destruction" (44 U.S.C. § 3552(b)(3)(A) [\[6\]](Sources.md#^s6)). A model being wrong, with no attacker and no security control defeated, is not an integrity failure.
- **Social engineering is an attack on authentication.** A person acting on an identity or instruction that wasn't verified counts as an authentication failure.
- **Resilience** is read as part of availability (recovering from attacks). Safety fallbacks do not count on their own. By the same rule, **cyber-physical containment is outside this paper's scope**: where a wrong model output drives an actuator ([[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]), the stop that fires is a safety supervisor or fallback, not an information-security control, so [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] records that route as uncontained by the seven frameworks and does not claim the safety layer for H1b.
- The definition must include phishing, business email compromise, ransomware, credential stuffing, insider misuse, supply-chain compromise, and DDoS. If it excludes one, the definition is wrong, not the concern.

**Known limitations of the method:**
- Classifications and control mappings are a **single analyst's** judgment. Have a second person classify a sample blind and compare. [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]]'s step cells have since had a blind second rating, with AI assistance rather than a person ([[RQ3 Second Rating]]).
- **H1b was restated after the [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] results were known.** As first worded, it said the gaps were "not in the control set's ability to contain impact." [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] found two paths ([[G42 CBRN information or capabilities|G42]]'s jailbreak route, [[G41 Physical safety (cyber-physical AI)|G41]]'s adversarial-sensor route) that no information-security control contains, which meets falsifier 5. The restated H1b names three exceptions (output as the harm, unintended permitted actions, wrong outputs to actuators). Like the per-role H1, it is a claim the data support, not a prediction the data confirmed.
- Counts in [[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]] are counts of **catalogue entries** as the source frameworks itemize them. For example, OWASP splits embedding attacks into five entries, which inflates Class C.
- **Partial routes.** 17 of the 52 in-scope concerns, and 2 of the 11 catastrophe scenarios, are analyzed only on the routes that require a security failure. Findings describe the security routes behind the concerns people raise, not every way those concerns could come about. The other routes are named in [[RQ2 Screening Log]].
- **H1 was restated per role after the per-role results were known.** Under the original combined test (fewer than half of catalogued risks in Class A or B), attacks on AI systems fall short on catalogue counts (9 of 23, 39%). The per-role hypotheses are claims the data support, not predictions the data confirmed; the H1.3 test in particular was written after its result was seen (now 22 of 27).
- [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] incident evidence is mostly **operator or vendor self-reported**. Absence-of-harm claims from affected parties ("no evidence of tampering") are weaker than positive findings.
- Five of the nine [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] full cases are **evaluation or training runs** that reached real systems, three of them with production safeguards stated to be off; four are operational deployments (Replit, GTG-1002, Cline, Grok/Bankr). The [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] register adds operational incidents with thinner records. [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] gives no rates: only published incidents can be counted, and operators publish selectively.

## Research Questions

1. How do AI systems differ from traditional computing systems in ways that matter for security?
2. Which claimed AI security risks are novel, and which are established risks relocated or amplified in tempo, frequency, or cost? (Rubric classes A–D)
3. For each novel (C/D) risk, and for each catastrophe scenario whose path runs through one, which established controls bound each step of the attack path, and where does an assurance gap remain with those controls fully in place? (Tests H1b.)
   - 3.1. Across the seven frameworks, what is the smallest set of requirements that produces that containment, and what lies beyond it?
4. In prominent "rogue AI" incidents, were the causal steps Class A/B failures or Class C/D mechanisms? And what does AI change about the *achievability* of full control implementation?

## Notes

AI for Security: Using AI to enable security teams to improve the security posture of an organization.

Security of AI: Safeguarding the models, data, and pipelines that power AI applications themselves from manipulation, data poisoning, or misuse.

The appropriate response to AI-driven risk amplification is often to strengthen existing security capabilities—identity, data protection, monitoring, resilience, supply-chain security, access control, and secure architecture—rather than defaulting to AI-branded point solutions. (Supported by [[RQ3 Control Coverage|RQ3]] pre-AI containment table and [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]] causal-step classification.)

### Working thesis

> Sorted by the role AI plays, the claimed AI security risks split sharply. When attackers use AI, every risk is an established attack amplified in tempo, frequency, or attacker cost (9 of 9). When AI is a faulty component in an organization's own systems, nearly every risk is an established failure, relocated or amplified (10 of 11). When attackers target AI systems themselves, fewer than half are established problems (12 of 27), and this is where the genuinely novel mechanisms sit [RQ2 tally by role], [\[32\]](Sources.md#^s32)–[\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34), [\[36\]](Sources.md#^s36). Established controls contain the impact of nearly all catalogued risks (28 of 33). Of the 52 concerns people raise that are in scope, 30 have a security route that has happened outside a controlled test (🟢), while the severe versions have not been shown: all 9 in-scope maximal paths and all 11 catastrophe scenarios fail at a step with no demonstration ([[RQ2 Screening Log]]). Measured data show the amplification most clearly in attacker cost; tempo and frequency trends are real, but the organizations measuring them do not yet attribute them to AI ([[RQ2 Risk Classification|RQ2]], Measured Amplification Evidence). The incidents publicly described as "rogue AI" were conventional control failures exploited at machine tempo ([[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents|RQ4]]). A small set of risks (prompt injection, adversarial inputs, learned backdoors, agentic intent propagation, and misaligned goals) is genuinely new. Existing controls can contain them but can't yet *prevent* them or give the assurance conventional software allows, and all but learned backdoors and misaligned goals have already been seen outside the lab. In the real incidents examined, prompt injection succeeded on its own only once. In GTG-1002, attackers jailbroke a hosted model with simple role-play and task-splitting, and the provider's guardrails failed. That case falsifies H1.3 for attacks where the attacker controls the deployment. It is the exception, not the rule: everywhere else a conventional failure was also needed. But it shows that prompt injection, direct and indirect, is where defensive effort on the AI itself belongs, and that when attackers run the model themselves, that effort falls on the provider. The reality is established risk, amplified, plus that short list of assurance gaps. The FUD is the claim that AI risk is new in kind, or catastrophic today.

**Tagline:** "When computing shifted from deterministic to probabilistic, so did security." Better, per [[RQ1 How AI Systems Differ|RQ1]]: "When behavior went from specified to learned, assurance became statistical." We've never had perfect security; with learned systems we also can't enumerate every behavior to test, which is exactly the Class D list.

**Title thought:** A title that calls concerns "FUD" presupposes the answer. Consider framing it as a question or a sorting task, e.g., "Separating FUD from Reality in AI Security."
