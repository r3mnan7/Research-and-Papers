## Table of Cyber Risks

The Class and Author Analysis columns are the human reviewer's final classes and reasoning, refined in discussion with the AI assistant ([[RQ2 Blind Classification Worksheet]]). | Training Manipulation / Data and Model Poisoning | Manipulation of training processes, training data, fine-tuning data, embeddings, model artifacts, or related data causes an AI system to learn or exhibit attacker-desired behavior. | Integrity; System Control | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[28\]](Sources.md#^s28), [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[97\]](Sources.md#^s97), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | Poisoning can occur during pre-training, fine-tuning, embedding creation, RAG, model distribution, feedback loops, or other stages where data or learned state is incorporated. | Supply-chain compromise; data-integrity compromise; malicious configuration or code modification | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Corrupting trusted data is old; corruption expressed as *learned behavior* is ML-specific. The risk is therefore Class C. Input controls (provenance, source trust, and data validation) together with evaluation contain it, but because models are commonly trained on scraped public data, those controls bound the risk rather than eliminate it [\[242\]](Sources.md#^s242). |
| Data Integrity / Context Risk | Data used by an AI system may be corrupted, stale, inappropriate, incomplete, or otherwise unsuitable for the intended operating context. | Integrity | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[28\]](Sources.md#^s28), [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98) | Model behavior depends directly on the quality, provenance, timeliness, and suitability of training, retrieval, and operational data. | Database and application data integrity | B — Relocated | Standard data-integrity and data-lifecycle problem with a new consequence (behavior, not just records). NIST lists stale/unrepresentative data as an AI difference [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), but the control set is existing data governance. | Although the category is broad, data-integrity failures are long established; the AI system is a new consumer and store of the affected data. An established weakness in a new place: Class B. |
| Model Evasion / Adversarial Inputs | An attacker crafts or modifies input so that a learned model produces an incorrect classification, prediction, or decision. | Integrity; System Control | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[59\]](Sources.md#^s59), [\[42\]](Sources.md#^s42), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | Inputs may be deliberately optimized against learned decision boundaries, including perturbations that remain innocuous or imperceptible to humans and physical-world manipulations of sensor input. | Input manipulation; detection evasion; sensor spoofing | D — Novel mechanism, assurance gap | Known since 2013 [\[42\]](Sources.md#^s42). Objective is familiar (wrong decision), but NIST states that designing models that resist evasion while keeping accuracy "remains an open problem" and that there are "no information-theoretic security proofs" for widely used ML algorithms [\[22, §2.2.5 and §1 respectively\]](Sources.md#^s22). Containment exists (don't let one prediction authorize a serious action); prevention guarantees do not. | Adversarial evasion resembles a logic flaw, but no flawed logic exists to correct: the model behaves as trained, and crafted inputs exploit its learned decision boundaries. Controls outside the model, such as not letting a single prediction authorize a serious action, can contain the impact, but none establishes that a crafted input will not succeed under normal operation; NIST describes evasion-resistant models as an open problem [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22). Class D. |
| Model Extraction | An attacker attempts to reconstruct or reproduce a model's functionality or internal characteristics through interaction or observation. | Confidentiality | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[44\]](Sources.md#^s44) | Repeated queries, logits, confidence information, timing, or other observable behavior may permit reconstruction of model functionality or characteristics. | Black-box reverse engineering; oracle attacks; side-channel analysis | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Demonstrated against production prediction APIs in 2016 [\[44\]](Sources.md#^s44). Uses learned-model query behavior (ML-specific mechanism), but the containment is established: rate limiting, output minimization, abuse monitoring. Classified the same as the other inference/inversion attacks for consistency. | Behavioral distillation through legitimate access requires no security failure and is outside scope. The in-scope routes are fraudulent or proxy accounts, an authentication and abuse problem regardless of the attacker's resources, and extraction of exact parameters through returned confidence scores [\[44\]](Sources.md#^s44). Withholding those scores is not security through obscurity: it removes a channel, applying least privilege to outputs. The mechanism, which exploits how a learned model answers queries, is novel, but identity verification, abuse detection, and output minimization contain it. Class C. |
| AI Model Theft | Threat actors obtain proprietary model artifacts through compromise, exploitation, insider activity, or exfiltration. | Confidentiality | [\[30\]](Sources.md#^s30), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98) | Trained model weights and associated artifacts may themselves constitute valuable proprietary information. | Network intrusion; data theft; intellectual-property exfiltration | B — Relocated | Exfiltration of a high-value file. RAND's weight-security playbook is built almost entirely from conventional security levels (access control, insider threat, physical, supply chain) [\[30\]](Sources.md#^s30). The valuable file happens to be a model. | Model theft is a conventional data-loss scenario applied to a new asset. No new control is required; existing data-protection controls must be properly applied to model weights and training data. An established weakness in a new artifact: Class B. |
| Membership Inference | An attacker determines whether a particular record or example was contained in training data or a retrieval corpus. | Confidentiality; Privacy | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[43\]](Sources.md#^s43) | Statistical behavior, model confidence, similarity scores, or response differences may expose the presence of data without directly exposing its contents. | Oracle attacks; side-channel inference; database inference | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Formalized in 2017 [\[43\]](Sources.md#^s43). Exploits a learned model's differential behavior on training members (ML-specific). Contained within a stated bound by differential privacy and output restriction (see [[RQ3 Control Coverage\|RQ3]]); not zero-disclosure. | Membership inference is specific to learned models: a model's differing behavior on its training members reveals whether a record was used. Differential privacy and output restriction bound the disclosure within stated limits. Class C. |
| Training Data Reconstruction / Gradient Inversion | An attacker reconstructs private training examples from gradients, parameter updates, or other artifacts produced during machine learning. | Confidentiality; Privacy | [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[53\]](Sources.md#^s53), [\[54\]](Sources.md#^s54), [\[45\]](Sources.md#^s45) | Federated and distributed learning may keep raw data local while sharing gradients or updates that can nonetheless contain enough information to reconstruct portions of the original data. | Side-channel disclosure; information reconstruction from derived artifacts | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Shown in 2019 [\[45\]](Sources.md#^s45). Same class as membership inference and extraction: the disclosure channel is ML-specific, and containment (differential privacy, secure aggregation, limiting who receives updates) is bounded rather than absolute. | Gradient inversion is the established problem of reconstructing source data from derived releases [\[243\]](Sources.md#^s243), [\[244\]](Sources.md#^s244), applied to the updates exchanged during training [\[45\]](Sources.md#^s45). Its channel is specific to learning, but differential privacy [\[15\]](Sources.md#^s15), [\[20\]](Sources.md#^s20) and secure aggregation [\[60\]](Sources.md#^s60) bound the disclosure within stated limits. Class C. |
| Availability Attacks Against AI Systems | Attacks intended to degrade, exhaust, or deny access to AI capabilities or supporting infrastructure. | Availability | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Expensive inference, accelerators, context processing, and model infrastructure create attractive exhaustion targets. | DoS; DDoS; resource exhaustion | C — Novel mechanism, contained | (AI class: B — Relocated.) An expensive computer can still be denied service like any other computer. | Conventional denial of service against AI infrastructure is established, and user budgeting, an established cloud practice, limits consumption. The distinctive route is exhaustion through the model interface itself: inputs crafted to maximize computation (energy-latency attacks) [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22) exploit the model's behavior rather than a software flaw. Quotas and budgets contain the impact. Class C. |
| Unbounded Consumption / Denial of Wallet | Attackers trigger excessive inference, tool execution, reasoning, or resource consumption that creates service disruption or disproportionate financial cost. | Availability; Resilience | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Token counts, reasoning depth, context size, multimodal processing, and recursive agent/tool execution may create significant attacker/defender cost asymmetry. | Algorithmic-complexity DoS; cloud-cost abuse; billing attacks; resource exhaustion | B — Relocated | (AI class: A — Amplified.) Algorithmic-complexity DoS and billing abuse at a larger attacker/defender cost asymmetry. Magnitude changes; category doesn't. Quotas, budgets, and rate limits are the established controls. | Resource exhaustion and billing abuse predate AI and were familiar from cloud computing. Token, reasoning, and tool costs make the AI system a new place for an established availability and cost problem, contained by quotas, budgets, and rate limits. Class B. |
| AI Supply Chain / Third-Party AI Technology Risk | Risk introduced by externally supplied models, datasets, adapters, frameworks, services, tools, or hosting infrastructure. | Confidentiality; Integrity; Availability | [\[1\]](Sources.md#^s1), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | AI expands software supply chains to include model weights, datasets, adapters, transformation pipelines, quantized artifacts, and model services. | Software and hardware supply-chain compromise | B — Relocated | Different components, same supply-chain problem. | This is SBOM and supply-chain risk applied to AI. |
| Model Artifact Provenance / Tampering | Model artifacts, adapters, datasets, or converted models may be maliciously replaced, altered, or impersonated. | Integrity; System Control | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25) | Unsigned artifacts, mutable model references, compromised model hubs, conversion pipelines, or artifact registries can deliver malicious models. | Malicious repositories; package substitution; artifact tampering | B — Relocated | New artifact type, old provenance and integrity problem (signing, hashing, registry control). | Provenance and tampering is chiefly a matter of SBOM and verification processes. Signing for authenticity and checksums for integrity apply to model files as they do to any other artifact. An established supply-chain integrity problem on a new artifact: Class B. |
| Evasion of Security Detection | Attackers modify malicious behavior to avoid security detection systems. | Integrity; Availability; System Control | [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34), [\[35\]](Sources.md#^s35), [\[97\]](Sources.md#^s97) | AI can accelerate mutation, testing, and adaptation against defensive systems. | Malware evasion; polymorphism; detection bypass | A — Amplified | Faster mutation and testing against defenses. Watch item: GTIG reports malware that queries an LLM at runtime to obfuscate itself (PROMPTFLUX, experimental) and one family observed in operations (PROMPTSTEAL) [\[35\]](Sources.md#^s35). Still detection evasion, but the most credible counter-evidence to "nothing new"; revisit as it matures. | Detection evasion is a long-established specialization of dedicated threat actors and groups. AI changes only its economics. |
| AI-Enhanced Phishing | Generative AI produces convincing or personalized phishing content. | Confidentiality; Authentication | [\[32\]](Sources.md#^s32), [\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34), [\[37\]](Sources.md#^s37), [\[97\]](Sources.md#^s97), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | AI reduces the time, expertise, and cost required for high-quality personalization and language generation. | Phishing; spear phishing | A — Amplified | Textbook amplification: NCSC assesses AI gives "capability uplift in reconnaissance and social engineering," making them "more effective, efficient, and harder to detect" [\[32\]](Sources.md#^s32). FBI: generative AI "reduces the time and effort criminals must expend" [\[37\]](Sources.md#^s37). Phishing remains phishing. | AI-enhanced phishing introduces nothing new. |
| Deepfake / AI-Enabled Impersonation | Synthetic text, audio, images, or video impersonate trusted persons and influence victims to disclose information or authorize actions. | Confidentiality; Authentication; Authorization | [\[37\]](Sources.md#^s37), [\[97\]](Sources.md#^s97), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | AI can cheaply reproduce voice, visual appearance, mannerisms, and other signals humans historically used to establish identity and trust. | Fraud; impersonation; social engineering | A — Amplified | A familiar voice or face was never an engineered authenticator; AI collapses the cost of forging an *informal* signal. The FBI's recommended controls are established ones: hang up and call back through a known number, and pre-agreed secret words [\[37\]](Sources.md#^s37). Same logic as agent persistence: the change is the cost of the attack, not the type of control needed. | Deepfake impersonation extends social engineering to its logical conclusion. AI collapses the cost of forging informal identity signals, such as a familiar voice or face, that were never engineered authenticators; the attack and its target are established, so the risk is amplified (Class A). Established verification controls address it: refusing unrecognized callers and confirming requests through an independent third party. Verification may need to become routine for certain conversations, and organizations may need to restrict permitted channels. Consumer settings are harder to protect, yet the same validation principles apply. |
| Autonomous or AI-Assisted Attack Tools | AI is used to automate, adapt, or execute malicious activity with reduced human intervention. | Confidentiality; Integrity; Availability; System Control | [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[33\]](Sources.md#^s33), [\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34), [\[36\]](Sources.md#^s36), [\[97\]](Sources.md#^s97), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | AI may increase attacker speed, scale, persistence, and adaptability. | Automated hacking; malware; attack frameworks | A — Amplified | Strongest single amplification datapoint: in the GTG-1002 espionage campaign, AI performed "approximately 80 to 90 percent of all tactical work," with "thousands of requests, representing sustained request rates of multiple operations per second," using conventional tools (scanners, password crackers) [\[36\]](Sources.md#^s36) (full report p. 7) (corrected Nov 5, 2026; see [[RQ4 Rogue AI Incidents]]). Tempo and frequency, not new technique. Same report: hallucinated credentials remained "an obstacle to fully autonomous cyberattacks" [\[36\]](Sources.md#^s36). | Autonomous attack tooling is, by definition, the attacker operating at greater scale. With stronger defenses, whether an AI or a human is at the keyboard is immaterial. |
| AI-Assisted Malware Generation | AI assists in producing or modifying malicious software. | Confidentiality; Integrity; Availability; System Control | [\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34), [\[35\]](Sources.md#^s35), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | Code-generation capabilities lower expertise requirements and accelerate malware creation or modification. | Malware development | A — Amplified | GTIG (Jan 2025): "productivity gains but not yet developing novel capabilities" [\[34\]](Sources.md#^s34). GTIG (Nov 2025) reports a "new operational phase" with LLM-querying malware, mostly experimental [\[35\]](Sources.md#^s35). Classified A today and flagged as the item most likely to move. | AI-assisted malware is the established malware problem at greater scale; nothing about it is new. |
| Sensitive Information Disclosure | Confidential, regulated, privileged, or proprietary information is exposed through model outputs, context, tools, logs, embeddings, reasoning traces, or other channels. | Confidentiality | [\[28\]](Sources.md#^s28), [\[29\]](Sources.md#^s29), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[98\]](Sources.md#^s98), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | AI introduces additional data-processing and derivation surfaces through prompts, RAG, model state, embeddings, memory, tool calls, and telemetry. | Data breach; information leakage | B — Relocated | More places to leak (prompts, RAG, memory, telemetry); disclosure remains an established confidentiality risk. | The disclosure itself is familiar; what is new is the vector of exposure, through prompts, retrieval, memory, and telemetry. An established confidentiality failure in a new place is Class B. Whether such a loss occurred can in principle be determined, and adequate measures likely exist. |
| Model Backdoor / Trigger Attack | Hidden learned behavior causes a model to produce attacker-selected results when a particular trigger is present while behaving normally otherwise. | Integrity; System Control; Confidentiality | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[55\]](Sources.md#^s55), [\[56\]](Sources.md#^s56), [\[47\]](Sources.md#^s47), [\[48\]](Sources.md#^s48), [\[49\]](Sources.md#^s49) | Malicious behavior can be encoded in weights, learned representations, or model architecture rather than a conventional executable code path and remain dormant until triggered. | Software backdoors; supply-chain compromise; logic bombs | D — Novel mechanism, assurance gap | Backdoored nets shown in 2017 [\[47\]](Sources.md#^s47). The gap is proven, not speculative: backdoors can be planted that are computationally undetectable [\[48\]](Sources.md#^s48); NIST, citing prior work, notes that backdoors can be "indistinguishable from naturally occurring features" [\[22, §2.3.3\]](Sources.md#^s22); and trained-in deceptive behavior can persist through standard safety training [\[49\]](Sources.md#^s49). For code you can at least inspect; for weights you can't establish absence. Supply-chain controls contain *who* can insert one, not *whether* one exists. | Backdoor insertion resembles supply-chain, third-party, data-integrity and insider risk, and restricting training-data access to legitimate parties controls who could insert one. Those controls govern origin, not behavior: most organizations receive models rather than train them, a signature establishes provenance rather than the absence of hidden behavior, and backdoors can be planted so as to be computationally undetectable [\[48\]](Sources.md#^s48). Containment exists, but no method gives assurance comparable to conventional software. Class D. |
| Direct Prompt Injection / Jailbreaking | A user supplies input that changes model behavior or bypasses intended restrictions. | Integrity; Authorization; System Control | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[22\]](Sources.md#^s22), [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[41\]](Sources.md#^s41), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | Current LLMs lack a hard architectural separation between instructions and data: both are represented in the same context and influence token prediction. | Input injection; confused deputy | D — Novel mechanism, assurance gap | SQL has a hard data/instruction distinction that parameterization enforces; current LLMs do not [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27). The model vendor itself states prompt injection "is unlikely to ever be fully 'solved'" [\[41\]](Sources.md#^s41). External permission checks contain it; nothing prevents it inside the model. | Direct prompt injection resembles command injection, but established defenses do not transfer: no equivalent of parameterization separates instructions from data inside a model [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27), and the vendor states the problem is unlikely ever to be fully solved [\[41\]](Sources.md#^s41). Defenses adapted from established principles, chiefly authorization enforced outside the model, contain its impact without giving assurance. Class D. |
| Indirect Prompt Injection | Malicious instructions embedded in external content are retrieved and interpreted by an AI system, changing its behavior without the legitimate user directly issuing those instructions. | Integrity; Authorization; System Control | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27), [\[25\]](Sources.md#^s25), [\[57\]](Sources.md#^s57), [\[58\]](Sources.md#^s58), [\[50\]](Sources.md#^s50), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | Untrusted web pages, email, documents, RAG content, tool output, images, audio, or memory can become behavioral instructions to an AI system operating with another principal's authority. | Stored XSS; input injection; confused deputy | D — Novel mechanism, assurance gap | Demonstrated against deployed LLM-integrated apps in 2023 [\[50\]](Sources.md#^s50). Data crossing one trust boundary becomes intent for an intermediary acting with another principal's authority. Out-of-model authorization contains *unauthorized* actions but can't distinguish attacker-shaped from user-intended actions *within* granted permissions (see [[RQ3 Control Coverage\|RQ3]]). | Indirect prompt injection resembles command injection, but the analogy fails at the fix: parameterization gives SQL a hard boundary between instructions and data, and language models have none [\[27\]](Sources.md#^s27); the vendor states the problem is unlikely ever to be fully solved [\[41\]](Sources.md#^s41). Containment exists today, through authorization enforced outside the model and isolation patterns such as an "airlock," in which a separate read-only agent screens incoming data as safe, unsafe, or for review before the working agent may use it. Such measures mitigate much of the risk but do not give assurance: the screening agent can itself be manipulated, and attacker-steered actions within granted permissions remain possible. Class D. |
| Excessive AI System Privilege / Agency | AI-enabled systems are granted functionality, permissions, or autonomy beyond what is required. | Authorization; Confidentiality; Integrity; Availability; System Control | [\[28\]](Sources.md#^s28), [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[26\]](Sources.md#^s26), [\[58\]](Sources.md#^s58), [\[52\]](Sources.md#^s52), [\[99\]](Sources.md#^s99) | AI systems may dynamically select tools and actions and can cause damage rapidly when given broad privileges. | Over-privileged service accounts; insider threat; excessive application permissions | B — Relocated | Least privilege has been a design principle since 1975 [\[52\]](Sources.md#^s52). Over-permissioning is not new; the over-permissioned principal is. | Excessive agency is a conventional identity and access management failure applied to a new kind of principal, the AI agent. Class B. |
| Agentic Authority / Intent Propagation | A multi-step or delegated AI workflow performs actions that are technically authorized but no longer legitimately derive from the user's original intent. | Authorization; Integrity; System Control | [\[57\]](Sources.md#^s57), [\[58\]](Sources.md#^s58), [\[51\]](Sources.md#^s51) | Probabilistic agents interpret goals, ingest external information, dynamically choose tools, and may delegate to other agents. Authority can therefore propagate farther than the human's original instruction while remaining technically permitted. | Confused deputy; delegated authorization; confused service account | D — Novel mechanism, assurance gap | The confused deputy is from 1988 [\[51\]](Sources.md#^s51), but a conventional deputy runs fixed code. An agent deputy's behavior is steered by the data it reads, so "does this identity have permission?" can't establish whether an action derives from the user's intent. Contained for enumerable permissions ([[RQ3 Control Coverage\|RQ3]]); the residual is harm *within* permission. This is the paper's most interesting finding. | The confused-deputy problem dates from 1988 [\[51\]](Sources.md#^s51), but a conventional deputy executes fixed code, whereas an agent's behavior is steered by the data it reads. Authorization can establish that an identity is permitted to act, not that a permitted action derives from the user's intent. Guardrails contain the risk but can themselves be fooled, and no method establishes how the agent will respond to every input. Class D. |
| Hidden Context Exposure | Attackers extract or infer non-user-facing instructions, tool schemas, policy logic, trust boundaries, or other implementation details from model context. | Confidentiality; Authorization; System Control | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | System prompts, developer instructions, tool descriptions, permissions, RAG policies, and workflow logic are often directly available to the model and therefore potentially discoverable. | Information disclosure; configuration leakage; reconnaissance | B — Relocated | New location for sensitive configuration; old confidentiality mistake. | Hidden context exposure is the established error of placing secrets where a client can reach them, relocated to the system prompt. Treating the prompt as exposed and keeping secrets out of it contains the risk on the relevant attack surface. Assurance in the rubric's sense means confidence comparable to conventional software, not certainty that no extraction could ever occur. Class B. |
| Vector and Embedding Weaknesses | Security weaknesses arise from embeddings and similarity-search mechanisms used in RAG, semantic search, agent memory, caches, and related systems. | Confidentiality; Integrity; Availability; Authorization | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Security behavior can depend on geometry in high-dimensional embedding spaces rather than deterministic database matching. | Data poisoning; inference attacks; side channels; multi-tenant isolation failures | Umbrella — not counted | Family heading for the five embedding rows below; excluded from counts to avoid double-counting. | Family heading; not counted. |
| Embedding Inversion | An attacker reconstructs original or approximate source information from stored embeddings. | Confidentiality | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23), [\[46\]](Sources.md#^s46) | Vector representations intended for similarity processing may retain sufficient information to reconstruct substantial portions of the data that generated them. | Information reconstruction; side-channel disclosure | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Text embeddings can be inverted to recover much of the source text [\[46\]](Sources.md#^s46). ML-specific channel; contained by treating embeddings as having the sensitivity of their source data (access control, tenancy separation). | Embedding inversion requires prior access to the vectors, whether through a breach, an exposed vector store, an interface that returns embeddings, or an insider, which is an established access-control failure. Its novelty lies in the finding that learned representations retain enough information to reconstruct much of their source text, a property of learned representations rather than a software flaw. Assigning embeddings the sensitivity of their source data and applying equivalent access controls contains the risk. The risk is therefore Class C. |
| Retrieval-Time / RAG Poisoning | Attackers craft data so that it is preferentially retrieved and supplied to an AI system as trusted context. | Integrity; System Control | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Content can be engineered specifically to occupy favorable positions in embedding space for targeted queries. | Data poisoning; search-engine manipulation | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Geometric manipulation of retrieval is ML-specific. Source whitelisting, provenance, and out-of-model authorization contain the effect; the instruction-following residual is the prompt-injection Class D item, not a separate gap. | RAG poisoning shares its delivery with training-data poisoning: placing malicious documents in a knowledge base is an established write-access and source-trust failure. Its novelty lies in the mechanism, since the attacker engineers content to win a learned similarity ranking for targeted queries, exploiting the model's learned judgment rather than a software flaw. Unlike training-data poisoning, the offending documents remain inspectable and removable, and source controls and monitoring contain the effect. The risk is therefore Class C. |
| Retrieval Jamming | Attackers manipulate retrieval behavior so targeted questions retrieve content that causes the system to refuse, fail, or provide no useful answer. | Availability | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Documents can be geometrically optimized for retrieval and exploit model behavior without containing explicit malicious instructions. | Denial of service; search poisoning | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Familiar availability objective via similarity-search manipulation; contained by source control and monitoring. | Retrieval jamming shares the delivery of RAG poisoning: the attacker must place a document in a source the system retrieves from, an established write-access failure. Its mechanism is novel: the document is engineered to win a learned similarity ranking and to trigger the model's learned refusal behavior without explicit instructions, evading injection filters. The result is a targeted denial of service achieved through learned behavior rather than a software flaw. Control over indexed sources and write access, together with monitoring for queries that begin to fail, contains it. The risk is therefore Class C. |
| Cross-Tenant Leakage Through Similarity Search | Shared vector indexes leak information about another tenant through similarity scores, result distributions, timing, or retrieval behavior. | Confidentiality; Authorization | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Similarity search may operate across a common vector space before access filtering occurs. | Multi-tenant isolation failure; side-channel inference | B — Relocated | Shared-resource inference and multi-tenant isolation failure; the vector system is a new signal source for an old problem. Fix is pre-filtering by tenant (established isolation). | Tenant leakage has been a concern since the advent of cloud computing. AI may require specific fixes, which may or may not yet exist, but the attack vector itself is not new. |
| Semantic Cache / Deduplication Poisoning | Attackers manipulate embedding-similarity thresholds so attacker-controlled content is reused, legitimate content is suppressed, or cache behavior is corrupted. | Integrity; Availability | [\[23\]](Sources.md#^s23) | Decisions about semantic equivalence are made through geometric thresholds that attackers can deliberately target. | Cache poisoning; data poisoning | C — Novel mechanism, contained | Cache poisoning via embedding-equivalence thresholds; contained by cache scoping and integrity controls. | Semantic cache poisoning belongs to the same family as RAG poisoning and retrieval jamming. The attacker engineers a query or content to fall within a learned similarity threshold, exploiting the model's learned judgment of semantic equivalence rather than a software flaw; this is its novel element. | **Counter-evidence:** NCSC, May 2025 [\[33\]](Sources.md#^s33) | Defenders who lag face "a digital divide"; keeping pace with frontier AI is "critical to cyber resilience" | Amplification can still be decisive. "Not new" ≠ "not serious." |

**Steelman note:** Amplification is not dismissal. A shrinking exploitation window [\[33\]](Sources.md#^s33) is an operational change defenders must meet. H1's claim is that the *remedy* is established controls executed faster and more completely, not a new security discipline.

**What the measurements say about H1.** Cost is the clearest measured amplification: phishing and exploitation are cheaper with AI in controlled settings. Success is mostly parity, not gain: AI phishing matched human experts, and two randomized trials found no significant uplift for people using chatbots, although agentic tools in 2026 may be changing that. Tempo and frequency trends are real, but the measuring organizations do not attribute them to AI: time to exploit was already falling before LLMs, and 2026 exploitation growth tracks disclosure volume. Measured AI shares of real-world activity are still small (about 2% of FBI complaints, about 10% of malicious email). So the evidence supports "amplified in cost" now and "amplified in tempo and frequency" only as an emerging trend. The paper should word H1 accordingly.
